The best video doorbell without monthly fee is the one that keeps a replayable clip without a cloud plan. The file has to land on hardware you control: memory inside the doorbell, a microSD card, or a hub or drive indoors. Ring’s support article says a subscription is required to review recorded video, and that without one you can watch live video and answer alerts as they happen. eufy, TP-Link’s Tapo line, Reolink, and Blink each publish a local path. Those paths are not the same purchase.

This is a roundup of the paths, from manufacturer pages fetched on November 3, 2026. It is not a scored test, and it does not crown a winner. ## How to choose the best video doorbell without monthly fee

Start with the clip, not the resolution. A sharp doorbell that only shows a live view is a different product from a sharp doorbell that still has Tuesday’s visitor on Wednesday.

Use the same five questions on the manufacturer page for the exact model, in this order.

Where does the file sit when you pay nothing extra? The honest nouns are on-device memory, a card, a hub, a recorder, or the company’s cloud. “Supports local storage” is not a location.

What still costs money? Cloud backup, a richer notification, a membership, and an extended warranty can sit beside a real no-fee recording path. Read the sentence about what works with no plan, not only the sentence about what the plan adds.

Is detection included? Person, package, vehicle, and pet tags are the features cloud doorbells most often move behind a plan. Some no-fee pages include the tags. Some include the tag and still charge for a snapshot inside the notification.

Can it record all day, and on what power? Continuous recording and a battery are usually enemies. If the page offers 24/7, read the power line and the chime line in the same breath.

Which radio does the porch actually have? Three of the pages below are 2.4 GHz only. A dual-band router that leaves the front door on a weak 2.4 GHz signal will look like a bad doorbell.

Write the answers down before you compare megapixels. If the worry is someone pulling the device off the wall, the [outdoor guide](/outdoor-security-cameras-without-subscription/) is the right next read. A doorbell is an outdoor camera with a button, mounted where a person can reach it.

## Do video doorbells need a subscription?

Some do, if you want history. Ring’s article “Understanding Ring Protect subscriptions” says a subscription is required to review recorded video on cameras and doorbells. Without a subscription you can view live video and respond to alerts as they happen. Events are recorded only after you subscribe. That sentence is why a cheap Ring doorbell is a poor answer to a no-fee search. The dated plan prices live on the Ring guide. This page will not retype them.

A video doorbell no subscription fee is the opposite design: the manufacturer says the clip can be saved without that plan. The phrase still does not mean “no account,” “no app,” or “no internet.” eufy’s video-doorbell collection says most video doorbells need Wi-Fi for live video, notifications, and reaching the footage from a phone, including doorbells that store locally. Blink’s Video Doorbell FAQ says setup needs a 2.4 GHz network, and that without Wi-Fi a press can still make a sound at the button while motion events are not recorded.

Optional cloud is the usual leftover, and it is not a scandal. It is a second product.

TP-Link’s Tapo D225 page lists a microSD card at no extra cost and Tapo Care as cloud storage you subscribe to separately. eufy’s local-security page markets no monthly fee and then asterisks it: if you turn on cloud backup, or push notifications with thumbnail previews, videos or thumbnail previews go to the cloud as needed for those features. Reolink’s support article says a Reolink subscription is not mandatory, and that microSD or FTP are the routes if you skip Reolink Cloud. Blink’s FAQ says you can use the doorbell without a Blink Subscription Plan, and that the no-fee save path is local storage on a Sync Module 2 with a USB drive sold separately.

“Subscription optional” is a second sentence you still have to finish. A page that only says the plan is optional may be describing a live view. Prefer the page that names where the file lives.

## What a subscriptionless video doorbell still has to include

Subscriptionless is the awkward word for the same job. You do not want a monthly archive fee. You will probably still create an account. The three ordinary places for the file are the ones the rest of this site already uses. A doorbell makes the theft problem obvious, because the camera is at hand height beside a door.

### Memory inside the doorbell

eufy’s US product page for the Video Doorbell E340 lists local storage as 8 GB on the device and says the doorbell works standalone or with a HomeBase that is not included. The FAQ on that page says 8 GB of eMMC can store 90 days of video if you assume 30 clips a day at 20 seconds each. That is ten minutes of events a day, not a continuous day. Do not stretch “90 days” into a promise about a busy street. The footnotes on the same page say thumbnail previews in push notifications are stored in the cloud temporarily, and that choosing cloud backup or those previews sends video or thumbnails to the cloud.

On-device memory is simple. It is also on the porch. If the doorbell leaves, the 8 GB leaves with it, unless a hub is in the path and the clips are actually going there. The page says HomeBase 2 and HomeBase 3 are compatible, and that HomeBase S380 needs firmware V3.3.2.6 or newer. Compatibility is not the same sentence as “the clip is indoors by default.”

### A card you can buy yourself

TP-Link’s Tapo D225 spec table lists a microSD slot up to 512 GB. The storage section says the card is purchased separately, and that Tapo Care is the subscribed cloud alternative. Reolink’s Video Doorbell WiFi spec table lists a microSD slot, maximum 256 GB, and says it works with a Reolink NVR. The product’s storage section also names a Home Hub and an FTP or NAS server. Reolink’s FAQ says the card is not included, and that 24/7 recording can go to the card or an NVR.

A card you can pull and open on a computer is a different feature from a card the app will play. Ask that before you buy. A card inside an outdoor doorbell has the same theft problem as eufy’s 8 GB chip. A card or a drive in a hub indoors does not.

The Tapo spec table lists the slot and does not say whether it is in the doorbell or in the plug-in chime. That gap matters. Open the diagram on the current page before you assume a stolen doorbell takes the archive. Reolink’s page labels a microSD card slot among the doorbell callouts and also offers the NVR, hub, and NAS routes. If you only insert a card, plan on that card living at the door.

### A drive that stays in the house

Blink’s Video Doorbell FAQ is blunt: the Video Doorbell has no way to store clips by itself. The no-monthly-fee route on that page is to add the doorbell to a Sync Module 2 system with a compatible USB flash drive, sold separately. Blink’s doorbell recording-settings page adds Sync Module XR as another local-storage route, and it adds a trap. With local storage, automatic Live View stops doorbell-press clips from saving locally, because Live View recording requires a subscription. Turning that feature off lets the press clips save, and it stops doorbell-press announcements on Echo devices. A roundup that only says “Blink can do local storage” has skipped the sentence that decides whether a press is on the drive.

The drive is indoors. That is the point. The doorbell still depends on Wi-Fi to record, per Blink’s outage answer. A recorder that keeps writing while the porch radio is dead is a different design. Blink is not describing that design for this doorbell.

## Four candidates, and the sentence each page actually commits to

The order below follows the storage patterns above. It is not a finish order. CameraReviewed has not mounted these doorbells for this draft. Prices move. One of the pages already disagreed with itself on price the day it was fetched.

### eufy Video Doorbell E340

eufy’s US listing is titled as a doorbell camera without a subscription. On November 3, 2026 the main price on that page read $169.99, and a comparison module on the same page read $149.99. This draft will not pick one of those numbers and call it the price. The same page listed an eufy Plus membership at $15 a month with cloud backup among the benefits. That membership is a bill. The 8 GB on the device is the no-fee path the spec table names.

The hardware story on the page, which you should re-read at checkout: two cameras, one aimed at the visitor and one down at the floor; 2K video; a 160 degree viewing angle; color night vision listed out to 16 feet; battery or wired power. Wired mode, the page says, still needs the battery installed, and the source should be 8 to 24 volts and greater than 10 VA. The doorbell can use an existing mechanical chime, a HomeBase, a Minibase Chime (T8023), Alexa, or Google. It is not compatible with the original eufy Wi-Fi Chime (T8020) or eufy Chime (T8740 or T8742).

The Wi-Fi FAQ on that page says the E340 does not support 5 GHz. It only supports 2.4 GHz. If the porch is far from the router, that single band is the install risk, ahead of the second camera.

eufy’s local-security page is the asterisk page. “No monthly fee” is followed by the cloud-thumbnail qualifier. Believe the qualifier. The collection page says a video doorbell without a monthly fee exists in the lineup and names the E340 and the S330 as models with built-in or HomeBase storage. This card is the E340 because the US product page states the 8 GB figure in public. It is not a claim that every eufy doorbell stores video in the same place. When the model is not the E340, use the storage table already cited from [security cameras without a subscription](/security-cameras-without-subscription/).

### TP-Link Tapo D225

TP-Link’s US D225 page is direct about the fee. It says on-device AI tags people, packages, and vehicles without a subscription. The spec table lists motion, person, pet, vehicle, and package detection. It also says a notification that includes a snapshot is a Tapo Care service. Free tags and paid snapshots are different lines. Activity zones are marked as working only with motion detection.

Storage is a microSD card up to 512 GB, bought separately, or Tapo Care if you want the cloud. The rechargeable battery is described as up to 8 months, with an asterisk that real life depends on settings, use, the router, and the environment. Do not treat 8 months as a measurement this site made. Hardwired mode is where the page puts uninterrupted 24/7 recording. That same hardwired section says the mode does not support use with an existing chime. The box contents include a Tapo chime and a jumper for an existing chime. Those two facts have to be reconciled in the quick start, not in a slogan. Read it before you assume the hallway bell still rings in the mode you wanted.

The radio is IEEE 802.11b/g/n at 2.4 GHz. The spec table has no 5 GHz line. The page says a chime is included, rates the doorbell IP66, and lists an operating range of -4°F to 113°F. Pre-roll is described as up to 4 seconds before the motion event. That short buffer is not the same thing as the hardwired 24/7 mode.

TP-Link’s page says the company participates in CISA’s Secure by Design pledge. That is TP-Link’s description of its process. It is not an audit of this doorbell, and it is not a reason to skip firmware updates.

### Reolink Video Doorbell WiFi

Reolink’s page for the Video Doorbell WiFi calls it a wired 5MP doorbell with a chime, dual-band Wi-Fi, and local storage with no monthly subscription. The spec table lists default video at 2560 by 1920, 5 megapixels, at 20 frames per second, and Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. Recording modes in the table are motion, which is the default, plus 24/7 and scheduled. The FAQ says there is no mandatory monthly subscription, and that person and vehicle detection, two-way audio, 24/7 recording, and local storage are available without hidden fees. A feature block also says the doorbell detects people, vehicles, and animals with no subscription fees. Those two sentences are close and not identical. Check the detection list on the current page before you count on the animal line.

Power is the section to read slowly. The spec table says 12 to 24V AC, 50/60 Hz, or 24V DC. One FAQ says the doorbell has no battery and must use the included adapter or existing 12 to 24V AC or 24V DC wiring. Another FAQ says it works with most doorbell power systems at 16V AC, 50/60 Hz, 30 VA, and tells you to ask a qualified electrician if you are unsure. A later answer mentions a 24V 0.5A adapter or existing 12 to 24V AC wiring. Those ranges are all on Reolink’s page, and they are not one sentence. They also do not match the 8 to 24V and greater-than-10 VA note on the eufy E340 page, or the transformer ranges on Ring’s hardwiring page. Use the page for the brand in your hand. The Ring install guide is a summary of Ring, not a universal voltage chart.

The chime copy on the same Reolink page disagrees with itself. A feature line says you can wire the doorbell to your existing mechanical chime. The FAQ says it does not work with other brands’ chimes or pre-existing mechanical chimes, that you bypass the old chime with the jumper in the box, that the old chime stops ringing, and that you use the included Reolink Chime. A later FAQ says the doorbell and its chime cannot work with doorbells or chimes from other brands. When a hero bullet and a FAQ conflict, do not let the hero bullet win by default. Plan on the included chime until the page says one thing.

The storage section names a microSD card up to 256 GB, a Reolink NVR, a Home Hub, or your own FTP or NAS server, with no mandatory fee. The spec table names the card slot and a Reolink NVR. The 24/7 FAQ limits that answer to the card, which is not included, or an NVR. If a Home Hub or a NAS is why you picked Reolink, confirm that route on the current page for this model. Reolink’s separate article, “Does Reolink Require Cloud Subscription for Recording?”, says the subscription is optional and points at microSD or FTP if you skip the cloud. That article is the brand policy. The doorbell page is the model policy. Read both.

The placement FAQ recommends mounting this model about 1.2 meters off the ground, and it says a 15 degree wedge is in the box. That is Reolink’s number for this doorbell. It is not a height rule for every brand. Do not carry it over to a Ring install.

This doorbell is hardwired only. That is why 24/7 is on the menu. It is a poor match if you have no doorbell wires and no acceptable place for the included adapter. Reolink’s offline FAQ is the clearest outage sentence in this roundup: without an internet connection, the doorbell can still detect motion or a person and record to the SD card, and it cannot send phone alerts or serve a live view. A connected Reolink chime can still sound when the button is pressed. “Without internet” is Reolink’s wording. It is not a claim about a porch with no Wi-Fi at all.

### Blink, and the box the listing leaves out

Blink is the candidate that makes “no monthly fee” do the most work, and the most damage, inside one FAQ.

The Video Doorbell FAQ says you do not need a Blink Subscription Plan to use the product. It then says you can save clips locally for no monthly fee by adding the doorbell to a Sync Module 2 with a USB flash drive sold separately. The next answer says the doorbell does not support local storage on its own. Without a Sync Module, live view and two-way audio exist only if you tap a motion or press notification within 60 seconds. With a Sync Module, those features are available on demand. A plan still exists. The FAQ describes cloud retention as 60 days per clip in the general case and 30 days in the EU and UK, plus a trial with a new doorbell. The plan is the cloud route. The USB drive is the no-fee route. They are not quiet versions of each other.

The same FAQ’s comparison block is why one “Blink doorbell” card is a hazard. A Wired Doorbell 2K+ row says a Sync Module is not required, and that the doorbell will not ring an in-home mechanical chime. Battery rows say a Sync Module is required, and that they can ring an in-home chime when wired. Match the row to the name on the box. This card means the no-fee local path the FAQ describes for the Video Doorbell: Sync Module 2 plus a drive. It is not a promise about every Blink doorbell on the shelf.

Wiring does not delete the batteries. The FAQ says that even when the Video Doorbell is wired, size AA 1.5-volt lithium non-rechargeable batteries power it, and that a mechanical or digital chime system must provide 16 to 24 volts AC at 40 VA maximum. Below 16 volts AC, the doorbell enters what Blink calls Event Response mode. Third-party wireless chimes are not compatible. Battery-life lines on the page, including “up to two years,” are Blink’s, and the page says settings, use, and weather change the result.

The recording-settings page is the other required read. Local storage there can be a Sync Module 2 or a Sync Module XR. Automatic Live View blocks local saves of doorbell-press clips. If you bought Blink so that a press would be on the USB drive, that toggle is the purchase.

## Camera doorbell no monthly fee

People also type “camera doorbell no monthly fee,” “doorbell camera without a subscription,” and “best doorbell camera no monthly fee.” Those are the same shopping job as this page: a camera at the door, a button, and no required monthly archive. A second URL for the shorter phrase would be two pages competing with each other. The hardware checks do not change when the word “video” drops out.

What does change is the mistake the shorter phrase invites. A camera doorbell can sound like any outdoor camera aimed at the porch. An outdoor camera does not have to ring a chime, share a transformer with a hallway bell, or sit at grabbing height for the same reason. If you do not need the button, use the outdoor guide and skip the chime problem. If you need the button, the power and the existing chime are part of the fee question. A doorbell that costs nothing per month and stays silent in the hallway is not the product the search described.

Video doorbell local storage is the phrase to take back to the product page. Search that page for “local,” then read the noun after it. Card, eMMC, HomeBase, Sync Module, NVR, FTP. If the only noun is cloud, you are on a subscription page, whatever the title says.

## What recording does when the network drops

Roundups often answer this with one cheerful sentence. The manufacturer pages do not, because the products do not work the same way.

Blink’s FAQ says the Video Doorbell needs working Wi-Fi to send notifications and to record motion events. Without that connection, a press can still play a sound from the doorbell, and motion events are not recorded until Wi-Fi returns. The clip of a person who arrived during the outage is not in Blink’s published design.

eufy’s collection FAQ says that when a doorbell camera loses Wi-Fi it cannot send real-time notifications or stream to a phone, and that eufy doorbells with local storage can still record events to an onboard card or internal memory during the outage. Live view and instant alerts wait for Wi-Fi. That is a collection-page answer, not a line this draft found repeated in the E340 product FAQ. Confirm it in the E340 manual before you treat an outage as covered. The E340’s own storage example remains an event budget, not a 24/7 claim.

Reolink’s offline FAQ, quoted above, says the Wi-Fi doorbell can keep recording to the SD card without internet, without alerts and without live view. That is the design to want if the failure you fear is “the ISP died and the only copy was supposed to be in the cloud.” It still needs the card, which is not in the box, and it still needs the doorbell itself to stay on the wall.

A hardwired 24/7 doorbell with a card or an NVR is the design that covers the gap between motion events. The Tapo page puts 24/7 on hardwired power only, and it says that mode does not support an existing chime. The Reolink Wi-Fi doorbell lists 24/7 because there is no battery to protect. eufy’s collection FAQ says most eufy doorbells record on motion or other events, and it names other models, the C31 and the S4, when the topic is continuous recording on wired power. Those are different products. They are not the E340 card.

## The chime and the transformer are part of the fee

A no-fee doorbell that cannot use the wires you have is a different project: a plug-in chime, a battery you will charge, and a porch that still has 2.4 GHz. That can be the right project. It should be a choice you make before the ladder, not a surprise on it.

These are pre-checks from the pages above. They are not a wiring procedure. This page does not assign wire colors. The Ring install guide explains why that would be a bad idea for Ring, and the same caution applies to every brand here. If you are not comfortable at the transformer, stop and hire an electrician.

eufy’s E340 page says wired mode keeps the battery installed and wants 8 to 24 volts and greater than 10 VA. An existing mechanical chime is listed as supported. Named eufy chimes are listed as not supported.

Tapo’s D225 page says the hardwired 24/7 mode does not support an existing chime. A Tapo chime is in the box. A jumper for an existing chime is also in the box. The quick start has to say which combination you are actually in.

Reolink’s Wi-Fi doorbell is hardwired only, ships with its own chime, and the FAQ says to bypass the old mechanical chime. The power lines on that page include both 12 to 24V AC and a 16V AC, 30 VA example. Read the current FAQ before you connect anything.

Blink’s Video Doorbell FAQ says 16 to 24 volts AC, 40 VA maximum, that the AA batteries stay in the doorbell even when it is wired, and that low voltage drops the doorbell into Event Response mode.

None of those sentences is permission to work on a live transformer. If the old doorbell is still a plain button and you do not know the VA rating, measure before you order.

## If someone takes the doorbell

Local storage answers the monthly bill. It does not, by itself, answer theft. The outdoor guide makes the same point for a camera under the eaves. A doorbell is easier to reach than an eave.

On-device 8 GB, or a card in the outdoor unit, leaves with the unit. A Sync Module’s USB drive, a HomeBase, an NVR, or a NAS can keep a copy inside, if the product is actually writing there and not only listing the option. eufy’s E340 page says standalone operation works, so the default archive can be the doorbell itself. Blink’s FAQ says the opposite: there is no archive until the indoor module and the drive are present. Reolink offers both, and the spec table’s firm line is the card plus an NVR.

Pick the failure you can live with. A stolen doorbell that held the only copy is a missing camera and a blank timeline. A stolen doorbell with the copy in a closet is a missing button and a surviving clip. Cloud history is the third failure: the company holds the file, you pay for that, and you have accepted their account. Ring is explicit that this is the design. It is a reasonable design when you travel and you will not maintain a drive. It is a bad surprise when you thought the doorbell you mounted had already solved the fee.
