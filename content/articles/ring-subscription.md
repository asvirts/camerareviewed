You do not need a Ring subscription to see who is at the door right now. You do need one if you want Ring to keep the video so you can watch it later. That split is Ring’s, from Ring’s support article on Protect subscriptions and from the plan page, not a guess added to make a buying guide sound decisive.

This page explains the tiers that were public on November 2, 2026, what the trial does, and when the rational move is to stop arguing with Ring’s model and buy a camera that stores video in the house. It is not a teardown of a doorbell. Prices move. Feature names move. Illinois, Texas, and other places restrict some recognition features; Ring’s footnotes say so, and you should read the footnote that matches your address instead of trusting a summary.

If you are about to wire a doorbell, the electrical steps are in [how to install a wired Ring video doorbell](/install-ring-video-doorbell/). Wiring does not answer the subscription question. Finish this page first if the bill is what you are worried about.

## The split, in Ring’s words

Ring’s article “Understanding Ring Protect subscriptions” says a subscription is required to review recorded video on cameras and doorbells. Without a subscription, you can view live video and respond to alerts as they happen. Events are recorded only after you subscribe. Motion videos in the cloud are stored for up to 180 days from the recording date. If you shorten or lengthen that window later, Ring says the new setting applies to future recordings, not to video already in the past, and past events you can no longer open stay unrecoverable.

The doorbell camera FAQ on ring.com says the same thing in a slightly friendlier register. Without a plan you still get real-time notifications and you can answer them. The dashboard can show the last image captured by active devices. You cannot review videos or images you missed, and you cannot save or share them. With a plan, motion videos are kept up to 180 days and photos up to 7 days, according to that FAQ. Those two retention numbers are easy to mix up. Video history and photo history are not the same clock.

So the practical test is: will you look at the door only when the phone rings, or will you want Tuesday’s visitor on Wednesday? If you will want Tuesday, budget a plan or buy a different storage design. Hoping the free tier secretly keeps a timeline is how people miss the one clip they cared about.

## The tiers listed on November 2, 2026

These figures are from ring.com/plans as fetched for this draft. They are not a promise about next month. Annual prices are the ones Ring showed next to the monthly prices. Plans auto-renew until canceled. One device or one location is covered per plan, not an unlimited personal fleet.

### Solo

Ring described Solo as video recording, smart alerts, and more, for one device, at $4.99 a month or $49.99 a year. The listed features included up to 180 days of video playback, person, package, and vehicle alerts, extended live view, and device modes. Alerts are marked as available for select devices. If you own exactly one doorbell and you want history, Solo is the tier Ring points at. The moment you add a second camera at that address, Solo is the wrong shape and Ring will ask you to move to a plan that covers the location.

### Multi

Multi was listed at $9.99 a month or $99.99 a year for all devices at a single location, with the same core recording and smart-alert story, plus an extended warranty note. Ring’s FAQ says Multi and Pro cover devices at one home, and a new device at a location that already has Multi is covered without a second Solo. If you know you will own a doorbell and a stick-up camera, price Multi first. Two Solos are not how the menu works.

### Pro

Pro was listed at $19.99 a month or $199.99 a year. Ring describes it as Multi’s coverage plus intelligent features and professional monitoring when an alarm is involved: video descriptions, Familiar Faces (marked beta and restricted in some places), 24/7 professional monitoring, and active warnings. The footnotes are the product. Familiar Faces is not available for customers in Illinois, Texas, and Portland, Oregon, because of local law, on the footnote Ring published. Several description features are unavailable in Illinois. If you live in one of those places, do not pay for a bullet you cannot turn on. If you do not own a Ring Alarm, read carefully whether the monitoring line is something your doorbell alone will use or something you are subsidizing.

### Virtual Security Guard

This was listed at $99 a month. Ring describes live agents who watch enrolled cameras, deter people, and can request emergency services, on top of the Pro feature set. It is a staffed service with eligibility limits, not a software toggle. It is also far outside the “I just want the porch clip” budget. Include it so the menu is complete, then ignore it unless you meant to hire that service.

### Add-ons that sit on top

Ring’s plan page also listed Pro Intelligence at an extra $5 a month per camera for features such as descriptions and Familiar Faces on one device, requiring Solo or Multi, and 24/7 continuous recording at an extra $3 a month per camera on eligible devices, with higher prices for some Elite models. Continuous recording was described as cloud storage up to 14 days and dependent on the network. That is a different product from event history. Event history saves the motions. Continuous recording saves the in-between. If your worry is the moment before the sensor tripped, continuous is the line to read, and it is not included just because you bought Solo.

Ring’s comparison table also includes a “Local Video Storage” row under advanced alarm features. This draft does not claim your doorbell records to a card, because the public pages above describe cloud history as the thing the plan unlocks. Open the current comparison and see whether the row is checked for your device before you tell yourself you already have a local archive.

## The trial, and the way people lose the clips

Ring says a free 30-day Protect trial starts when you set up a new or certified refurbished compatible device, accessories excluded, unless that location already has a trial or a Multi, Pro, or Virtual Security Guard plan. During the trial, Ring says you can save, share, and replay, and you get intelligent features such as video descriptions and Familiar Faces. Costco purchases may get a longer trial with the same kind of benefits. Retailer cards change. Read the one in your box.

When the trial ends and you do not subscribe, Ring’s FAQ says you lose the stored recordings and they will not be retrievable. That sentence is the whole argument for deciding during the trial, not after. Calendar the end date. Watch whether you actually opened history or only answered live calls. If you only answered live calls, you have evidence you may not need the plan. If you opened history twice and were glad, you have evidence you do.

Shared users cannot always buy the plan. Ring says the owner who set up the device has to subscribe. If you moved into a house with a doorbell already on someone else’s account, you do not have a plan problem yet. You have an ownership problem. The transfer is a Ring account task, not a setting in this guide.

## What the plan does not change

The plan does not change the pixels of the doorbell you bought. It does not run a cable. It does not fix a weak Wi-Fi signal at the porch. Extended live view and multi-camera live view are plan features Ring lists, so the length of a live session can change, but the lens does not. If the picture is bad, a subscription will not focus it.

The plan is also per location. Ring is explicit that two addresses need two plans. A cabin and a house do not share Multi.

Payment is card or the methods on ring.com. Ring says it does not take cash, checks, or money orders for Protect, and mailed payments can lapse the coverage. That is a dull sentence and a real way to lose history by accident.

## When you should not buy the plan

Buy the plan if you want Ring’s cloud timeline, the smart alerts on a supported device, or alarm monitoring you have actually read.

Do not buy the plan if you wanted a file in the house and you are willing to change hardware. Do not buy Pro for a face-recognition feature your city does not allow. Read the footnote.

Do not keep paying Solo out of inertia after you added two more cameras and those cameras are dark in the timeline. That is the most common quiet waste, and Ring’s own “what happens when I add a device” answer is the fix: move to a location plan.
