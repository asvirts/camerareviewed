You can buy a home security camera that records without a monthly subscription. The useful version of that sentence is narrower than the ad. The camera, or a box next to it, has to store the file. You will probably still create an account. You will probably still want the internet for a notification. And a sticker that says “no fee” does not tell you whether person detection, cloud backup, or a warranty extension costs extra.

This is a buying guide for that narrower question. It is not a lab test. CameraReviewed has not scored these cameras in a hallway. The names below are candidates Andrew Virts still needs to verify. Where a company states a storage fact on a public page, the page is linked. If you only have time for one related read, make it the outdoor version, [outdoor security cameras without a subscription](/outdoor-security-cameras-without-subscription/), or the network version, [security cameras that work without Wi-Fi](/security-cameras-without-wifi/).

## What people mean by no subscription

Shoppers usually mean one of four things, and they are not the same purchase.

First, they do not want a bill to look at yesterday’s motion clip. That is a storage question.

Second, they do not want a contract with a monitoring center that calls the police. That is an alarm question. A camera can be subscription-free and still be a bad fit for someone who wants professional monitoring. Ring’s higher plans are partly that service. Read [Do you need a Ring subscription?](/ring-subscription/) if that is the brand already on the door.

Third, they do not want any account. Almost none of the popular Wi-Fi cameras grant that wish. The app is the account.

Fourth, they do not want the camera to use the internet at all. That is possible with a recorder on the local network, and it is a different project. It is also easy to confuse with “no monthly fee.”

Write down which of the four you mean before you compare resolution. A 4K camera that only keeps clips in a paid cloud does not answer the first meaning, no matter how sharp the still looks.

## Where the video actually goes

There are three ordinary places for the file. Each one trades convenience against the chance that a thief, a dead card, or a dead drive takes the evidence with it.

### A card in the camera

A microSD card is the smallest system. The camera writes clips to a card you can pull out and read, at least in theory. Some apps make that awkward on purpose and prefer you to view clips in the app. Ask, before you buy, whether you can take the card out and open the files on a computer without the vendor’s software.

The failure mode is physical. The card is in the camera. If the camera is outdoors and someone takes it, they take the archive. Cards also wear out. A camera that writes constantly will kill a cheap card faster than a camera that writes short motion clips. Format the card in the camera if the manual says to, and replace it when the camera starts reporting errors instead of waiting for a blank day.

### A hub inside the house

eufy’s public local-security page describes storage on the device or on a HomeBase, and it puts asterisks on the promise. The support article “Storage Methods Compatibility” is more useful than the slogan, because it does not pretend every eufy product stores video in the same place. Some families record on the HomeBase. Some SoloCams record on the camera. Some doorbells record on the doorbell, some on a chime that needs a card. That table is the kind of page worth opening on a laptop before checkout, not on a phone in a store aisle.

A hub is plugged in, which matters during an outage only if the hub and the network are on a battery backup you actually own. Otherwise “local” still stops when the power stops. The hub is also one box to steal, but it is inside, which is a better bet than a camera on a fence.

### A recorder and a cable

An NVR, network video recorder, plus cameras on Ethernet is the old shape of a camera system, and it is still the clearest way to keep days of video without a cloud plan. Reolink’s local-storage page describes microSD, Home Hub, and NVR as the places a clip can sit, and it says a subscription is not required for that local storage. It also says remote viewing and phone notifications usually need an internet connection. Both sentences can be true.

The cost is installation. Someone has to run cable, or you buy a kit and accept the cable routes that are easy rather than the camera angles that are perfect. Power-over-Ethernet means the cable carries power and data, so you are not also hunting for an outlet at the eave. Channel count and drive size are the spec lines that decide whether the kit still works in six months. A drive that is “included” and tiny will overwrite Monday by Thursday if you record continuously.

## What still costs money

Free recording is not free hardware, and it is not always free software.

The camera costs money. A larger card or a drive costs money. If you want clips from a phone while you are away, you are using your home internet and, often, the vendor’s relay servers. Those servers can stay free, become limited, or pick up a fee later. The honest check is the current account page for that brand, not a review from last year.

Detection is the usual paid extra. A camera can record every motion clip locally and still charge to label the clip “person” or “package,” or to send a richer alert. If the only reason you wanted a camera was fewer false alarms from the tree, read that line on the spec sheet. “No monthly fee” beside “basic motion” can hide “person alerts sold separately.”

Extended warranty and cloud backup are also optional bills. They are reasonable products. They are not the same thing as the camera working. Keep them out of the comparison until the storage path is settled.

## How to read the spec sheet

Use the same five lines this site’s comparison table uses, in this order.

Subscription required. Look for a sentence about what happens with no plan, not a sentence about what the plan adds. Ring’s support article on Protect subscriptions says a subscription is required to review recorded video, and that without one you can view live video and respond to alerts as they happen. That is a clear sentence. Prefer brands that will write one.

Local storage. The noun matters: card, hub, NVR, or “cloud only.” “Supports up to 128 GB” is not the same as “includes a card.”

Resolution. Note the number and the lens. A 4K sensor with a very wide view can look softer on a face at the sidewalk than a narrower 2K camera. Do not collect megapixels as a score.

Power. Battery, plug, doorbell transformer, or PoE. Battery life claims assume a climate and a traffic level you may not have. [Outdoor cameras](/outdoor-security-cameras-without-subscription/) go further on weather and theft.

Price tier. In this draft, price tiers are labels, not quotes. Check the listing the week you buy. A kit that looks expensive can be cheaper than three cheap cameras plus a year of plans, and the reverse is also common.

## Accounts, firmware, and the network

Local storage does not retire security maintenance.

CISA’s 2021 advisory on certain Reolink P2P cameras described serious issues, including a hard-coded key and cleartext transmission, and pointed people at firmware updates, disabling P2P where possible, and keeping the devices off the open internet. That advisory is old. It is not a claim that a camera you buy this month is still vulnerable. It is a claim that “stores video at home” and “safe to expose to the internet” are different sentences. Apply the same suspicion to every brand, including the ones with friendlier marketing. Change default passwords. Turn off features you do not understand. Update firmware when the vendor ships a fix. Do not port-forward a recorder to the whole internet because a forum post from 2016 said to.

eufy’s local-security page talks about encryption and on-device analysis. Read it as the company’s description of its design, including the asterisks, not as an audit. If a page says thumbnails may still go to the cloud when you choose that option, believe the qualifier.

## When a subscription is the better answer

A monthly plan is the right tool in a few ordinary cases.

You travel and you will not maintain a drive. You want a company to store the clip somewhere the burglar cannot carry out. You already own Ring or a similar cloud camera and the plan is cheaper than replacing three devices. You want professional monitoring of an alarm, which is a service with people on the other end, not a feature of a memory card.

None of those cases is a moral failure. The mistake is paying for cloud history when you thought you were paying for a camera that already kept history, or skipping a plan and discovering the first real event was never saved.

If you are on the fence with a Ring doorbell specifically, the plan page and the support article are enough to decide. This guide will not invent a winner.

## A buying order that survives the ads

Decide the room or the door first, then the storage place, then the power, then the brand. People do it backwards because the brand ad is what ranked.

1. Write the job in one line. “See who is at the front door and keep three days of motion” is a job. “Best camera” is not.
2. Pick card, hub, or NVR. If the camera is outdoors and easy to reach, do not leave the only copy of the video inside it.
3. Match power to the mount you will really use. A battery camera you will not charge is an ornament.
4. Read the no-plan sentence on the manufacturer’s site the same day you buy.
5. Only then compare resolution and the shape of the notifications.
6. Check that someone in the house can export a clip without calling you.

The [no-subscription hub](/no-subscription/) links the guides that follow from that order. Indoor cameras are still thin on this site; the same storage rules apply and the privacy stakes are higher because the lens faces a room people use.
